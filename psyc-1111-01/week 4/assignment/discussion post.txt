How Illness Beliefs Shape Health Attitudes and Medical Care Decisions
Illness beliefs play a very important role in how people think about their health and decide when to get medical help. These personal ideas about sickness affect how people understand their symptoms, choose treatments, and work with doctors and nurses (Ogden, 2019). Research shows that what people believe about illness often matters more than what doctors tell them, which can create problems for getting good healthcare.
These beliefs matter in many ways. They decide whether people think their symptoms are serious enough to see a doctor, whether they will follow treatment plans, and whether they will keep taking medications long-term (Dawkins et al., 2021). People who think illness comes from personal weakness or punishment may feel ashamed and wait too long to get help. Those who see illness as a medical problem that can be treated are more likely to get help quickly. These beliefs also affect whether patients take their medications correctly, since people who doubt that medicines work or worry about side effects often stop taking them without telling their doctor.
Additionally, illness beliefs change how much people use healthcare services. Some people seek too much medical care because they worry constantly about their health, while others avoid needed treatment because of fear or cultural ideas about what causes sickness (Keshavarzi et al., 2022). This makes understanding and working with illness beliefs very important for good healthcare and better health outcomes.
Using Leventhal's Self-Regulatory Model for Diabetes Management
Sarah, a 35-year-old woman who just learned she has diabetes, will go through the stages of Leventhal's Self-Regulatory Model in ways that will greatly affect how well she manages her health. Each stage gives us important information about how she understands and handles her diabetes.
How Sarah Thinks About Her Illness: Sarah develops detailed ideas about her diabetes in five main areas. Her illness identity includes both the word "diabetes" and symptoms like needing to urinate often, feeling thirsty, and being tired. At first, she may have trouble connecting invisible problems like high blood sugar to her diagnosis. Her timeline beliefs change from hoping diabetes might go away to understanding it lasts for life and needs constant care. Consequence beliefs include both immediate worries about changing her lifestyle and long-term fears about problems like going blind or needing leg surgery. Her cause beliefs might include ideas about family history, lifestyle choices, or stress, which affects how responsible she feels for managing the condition. Finally, her control beliefs decide whether she thinks diabetes can be managed well through medicine, diet, and exercise (Ogden, 2019).
How Sarah Handles Her Diabetes: Sarah uses both problem-solving and emotion-focused ways to cope based on her illness beliefs. Problem-solving coping includes learning to check blood sugar, changing what she eats, starting exercise routines, and taking medications on schedule. She develops practical skills for managing diabetes every day, like counting carbohydrates and recognizing when her blood sugar gets too low. Emotion-focused coping deals with her feelings about the diagnosis, including worry about future problems, sadness about lifestyle changes, and concerns about what others might think. She might use strategies like getting support from family and friends, practicing relaxation, or changing negative thoughts to manage diabetes-related stress.
How Sarah Judges Her Success: Sarah constantly checks whether her coping strategies are working through different ways of getting feedback. She monitors blood sugar levels, energy levels, and symptom improvements to see if her management approach is effective. This evaluation affects whether she continues current behaviors, asks for changes to her treatment plan, or develops new coping strategies. When things go well, it strengthens positive health behaviors, while perceived failures may lead to strategy changes or visits to healthcare providers.
These stages work in a cycle rather than happening once, with each stage affecting and changing the others as Sarah gains experience managing her diabetes. Her illness beliefs change based on how well her coping works and what she learns from checking her progress, creating an ongoing process that shapes how successfully she manages her diabetes long-term.
Major Barriers to Getting Medical Help and Ways to Fix Them
In my community, many connected barriers prevent people from getting healthcare when they need it, requiring complete approaches to fix them effectively.
Money Problems: Many families cannot afford doctor visits, medical tests, prescription drugs, or health insurance payments. People often wait until they feel very sick, hoping symptoms will go away on their own. Solution: Creating payment programs based on income, expanding Medicaid coverage, and building community health centers with lower-cost services can reduce money barriers.
Distance and Transportation Problems: People in rural areas face long distances to healthcare facilities, with specialist doctors requiring lengthy travel. Limited public transportation and unreliable personal cars make these problems worse. Solution: Developing mobile health clinics, video doctor visits, and community transportation programs can improve healthcare access.
Cultural and Language Problems: Cultural beliefs about what causes illness, especially mental health conditions, create shame that prevents help-seeking. Language differences and confusing medical terms make communication with providers difficult, leading to misunderstandings and people not following treatment plans (Keshavarzi et al., 2022). Solution: Providing cultural training for healthcare providers, hiring bilingual staff, and developing health education materials that respect cultural beliefs can address these barriers.
Healthcare System Problems: Long waits for appointments, not enough providers, and complicated insurance procedures discourage people from using healthcare. Poor communication between providers and patients and rushed appointments leave patients feeling ignored or misunderstood. Solution: Adding more providers, offering same-day appointments, and training providers in better communication can improve healthcare access and quality.
Knowledge and Understanding Problems: Limited understanding of symptoms, when to seek care, and how to use healthcare systems prevents appropriate help-seeking behavior. Solution: Creating community health education programs, developing easy-to-understand health materials, and training community health workers can improve health knowledge and encourage timely healthcare use.
These solutions must be used together to address how healthcare barriers connect to each other and ensure fair access to quality medical care for all community members.
